2. WHAT THESE NUMBERS ALREADY SETTLE, BEFORE ANY LOAD IS SENT

2.1 THE RECORD CAP BINDS FOR COMPACT RECORDS; MAX_BODY_BYTES FOR FAT ONES. [derived]
A compact generated record - {"region":"West","amount":123.45,"sale_date":
"2026-01-01","order_id":"000000000001"} - is 84 bytes, 85 with its separating
comma, so body(n) is about 85n + 13 bytes.
  n =  1,000  ->     85,013 B  ~=  83 KiB
  n = 50,000  ->  4,250,013 B  ~= 4.05 MiB, well under the 10 MiB body cap.
  10,485,760 / 85 = 123,362 records, so for compact records the 50,000-record cap
  (api/main.py:115) binds first.
At maximum field widths a serialized record is at most 236 bytes: 64 + 32 + 64
characters of string content, plus at most 24 characters of float repr (Python
and serde both emit shortest-round-trip, bounded at 24; inf and nan are blocked
by allow_inf_nan=False), plus keys, quotes, colons and commas. 10,485,760 / 237
is about 44,240 records, so for maximal records MAX_BODY_BYTES binds first. Both
caps are reachable; which one you meet depends on record width, and the load test
should state which one it exercised.

2.2 MAX_S3_BYTES CANNOT BE REACHED THROUGH /jobs. AT ALL. [derived]
The stored object is submission.model_dump_json().encode("utf-8")
(api/main.py:209), i.e. the compact re-serialization of the validated model. A
serialized record is at most 237 bytes including its separator (2.1), and the
record cap is 50,000, so the object is at most 11,850,000 B = 11.3 MiB - always
below the 15 MiB cap in worker/worker.py:62. The worker's guard defends against
objects placed by some path other than the API. This load test cannot exercise
it and must not be reported as having done so.

2.3 NOTHING IN THIS SYSTEM RETURNS 503 ON /jobs. [derived]
See 3.2 for the full argument and its consequences for the counter design.

2.4 THE CONNECTION BUDGET IS NOT THE RDS CEILING. [derived]
api 15 per pod x 10 pods = 150; worker 7 per pod x 20 pods = 140; total 290 with
both autoscalers at maximum, plus superuser_reserved_connections (default 3) and
any psql pod. Against approximately 410-450 that leaves roughly 30-35% headroom.
RDS will therefore appear as LATENCY, not as refused connections. And because
pool_pre_ping=True on both engines (api/main.py:63, worker/worker.py:84), each
request costs an extra SELECT 1 round trip: the DB sees about 2 statements per
/jobs and about 3 per processed message. That doubling, not the connection count,
is what makes RDS latency the risk worth watching.

2.5 KEDA'S maxReplicaCount BINDS WELL BEFORE THE SPOT POOL'S CPU LIMIT. [derived]
20 workers x 500m = 10,000m of pod requests. Even on 4-vCPU nodes that is 3-4
nodes, i.e. 12,000-16,000m of node capacity, against the spot pool's 32,000m
limit. The spot pool will not be the constraint. KEDA's 20 will.

2.6 ON THE API SIDE THE HPA MAX AND THE NODEPOOL CPU LIMIT NEARLY COINCIDE, AND
WHICH ONE BINDS IS DECIDED BY KARPENTER'S INSTANCE CHOICE. [derived]

2.7 QUEUE DEPTH IS NOT A KNOB YOU CAN SET. [derived]
KEDA's aws-sqs-queue scaler counts visible plus in-flight by default

so the KEDA axis is driven by message rate versus per-message time - never set
directly. If the observed depth diverges from 4W, the scaleOnInFlight assumption
is wrong, and that is worth knowing.

3. THE THREE DESIGN POINTS CARRIED INTO THIS PLAN

3.1 THE HTTP CLIENT MUST DISABLE KEEP-ALIVE - AGREED, WITH TWO ADDITIONS.
The mechanism is right. EKS kube-proxy in iptables mode makes its DNAT decision
at connection establishment, selecting randomly across the ready endpoints, so a
keep-alive client pins its traffic to whichever pod answered first and pods the
HPA adds never receive anything - which would silently invalidate the whole test.
[verify] the mode once (section 14); iptables is the EKS default, and IPVS or
nftables behave the same way for this purpose.

Two things to carry alongside it.

(a) The Service has NO sessionAffinity (03-api-deployment.yaml:80-90 - the field
is absent, so it defaults to None). That is the OTHER way this test could
silently pin, and it is currently safe. Assert it in the run manifest so a future
change to it cannot invalidate a comparison against this run's numbers.

(b) The cost lands on conntrack, not on client ports. With Connection: close,
uvicorn is the ACTIVE closer, so TIME_WAIT accumulates on the api pods - where
the local port is fixed at 8000, so there is no port exhaustion - while the
generator's sockets are freed promptly. What does accumulate is conntrack entries
on the generator's node: at 900 connections/s with a 60s TIME_WAIT that is about
54,000 entries against nf_conntrack_max of 32,768 x 4 cores = 131,072 [derived]
[verify], roughly 2.4x headroom. node-exporter already exposes
node_nf_conntrack_entries and node_nf_conntrack_entries_limit, so this costs no
new tooling. Put both series in the timeline.

CONFIRMED BY MEASUREMENT, 2026-09-13. [measured] The active-closer half of that
paragraph held exactly. At 4,000 fresh connections/s against a local target,
with the client resolving each request when the server closed rather than
closing first:

  ss -tan state time-wait
    server side (local port is the listener)   14,107
    client side (peer port is the listener)         0

So TIME_WAIT lands entirely on the listener, the client's ephemeral ports are
freed as soon as the socket closes, and there is no client-side port exhaustion.
This is a property of the client, not a law: if the generator ever calls close()
before the server's FIN it becomes the active closer and 4,000 conn/s x 120s =
480,000 client sockets in TIME_WAIT against roughly 28,000 ephemeral ports.
loadtest/loadgen.py resolves every request in connection_lost for exactly this
reason, and that is not a detail to refactor away.

CORRECTED, 2026-09-13. [was] "at 900 connections/s with a 60s TIME_WAIT that is
about 54,000 entries ... roughly 2.4x headroom". Two errors, one in the constant
and one in the method.

  1. The timeout is 120s, not 60s. nf_conntrack_tcp_timeout_time_wait defaults
     to 120. kube-proxy overrides only --conntrack-tcp-timeout-established and
     --conntrack-tcp-timeout-close-wait; it never touches time_wait.
  2. rate x ttl only describes a steady state that has run longer than the
     timeout. Entries alive at t are every connection opened in (t - 120, t],
     which for a ladder of 45s rungs spans two or three EARLIER rungs as well.
     Any rung shorter than the timeout has to be integrated, not multiplied.

Redone: Stage B's 900 req/s top step is 900 x 120 = 108,000, which is 82% of the
131,072 table - not 54,000 and not 2.4x headroom. It still fits, but the margin
is 1.2x, and Stage A does not fit at all; section 5 has that derivation and the
ladder it forced. nf_conntrack_max itself is unchanged and still [derived]
[verify]: kube-proxy's conntrack maxPerCore default 32,768 x 4 vCPU = 131,072.

The measurement distortion is one extra TCP handshake per request, about
0.2-0.5 ms intra-VPC [estimate], against roughly 40 ms of S3/SQS/DB work per
request - under 2%, acceptable.

And the payoff: because every request is a fresh connection, the per-pod split is
directly provable two independent ways -
  sum by (pod) (rate(http_requests_total{handler="/jobs"}[1m]))  in Prometheus, and
  distinct destination IPs per minute in VPC flow logs. The generator is on the
  monitoring node and api pods are on Karpenter nodes, so the traffic always
  crosses an ENI and is therefore logged (traffic_type ALL,
  flowlogs_network.tf:44). That is the direct evidence that the keep-alive
  decision worked, rather than an assumption that it did.

3.2 TRACK 503 AND 500 AS SEPARATE COUNTERS - THE SPLIT IS RIGHT, THE PREMISE IS
NOT, AND THE REFINEMENT MATTERS.

I disagree that a 503 is available as a backpressure signal here, because nothing
in this system can return 503 on /jobs.
  - api/main.py:204-243 returns 202, 500 ("could not record job") and 502
    ("could not store batch", "could not enqueue job").
  - The body middleware returns 411, 400 or 413 (api/main.py:151-165).
  - FastAPI returns 422 through the custom handler (api/main.py:178-181).
  - The ONLY 503 in the codebase is /readyz (api/main.py:201).
  - uvicorn emits 503 only under --limit-concurrency, which is not set
    (api/Dockerfile.api:18).
  - There is no ALB, no ingress controller and no service mesh. The Service is
    ClusterIP (03-api-deployment.yaml:80-90) and the generator addresses it
    directly. docs/DESIGN.md:144-152 records the public entry point as
    deliberately removed.
  - If every api pod went NotReady, kube-proxy with zero endpoints REJECTs the
    connection. It does not synthesize a 503.

So in this system the backpressure signals are:
  - CONNECT TIMEOUT - uvicorn's accept backlog (default 2048) is full and the
    kernel is dropping SYNs. This is the genuine "offered load exceeds capacity"
    signal, and it carries no HTTP status at all.
  - 500 WHOSE LOG LINE CONTAINS "QueuePool limit of size 5 overflow 10 reached,
    connection timed out" - SQLAlchemy's default 30s pool_timeout expiring
    (api/main.py:61-62, :227). That is SATURATION, not a bug, and it arrives as
    a 500.
  - 500 FROM ANYTHING ELSE - that is a bug, and cutting the rate will not fix a
    bug. The distinction is the whole point of the split, so it has to survive
    into the data.

Which means the counter design must be richer than 503-versus-500, and it must
live on the client:
  - one counter per EXACT status: 202, 400, 411, 413, 422, 500, 502, other;
  - one counter per TRANSPORT failure class: connect-refused, connect-timeout,
    read-timeout, connection-reset;
  - one counter named offered_dropped - requests the generator wanted to send but
    could not because its in-flight cap was reached (section 6). Without this,
    "we could not offer the rate" gets misread as "the system handled the rate";
  - and the 500s get split AFTER the run by grepping the api's own log lines in
    CloudWatch for QueuePool: saturation-500 versus bug-500.

Server-side metrics cannot do this job. prometheus-fastapi-instrumentator 7.0.0
(api/api_requirements.txt:7) is created bare - Instrumentator().instrument(app)
at api/main.py:148 - so metrics.default() applies, and its should_group_status_codes
defaults to True, which means http_requests_total very likely carries
status="5xx" rather than "500", collapsing 500 and 502 into one series. [verify]
with the single command in section 14. This is consistent with the alert rule
itself, which matches status=~"5.." (13-prometheus-rules.yaml:42) - a pattern
that matches both "500" and "5xx". Either way, the client-side counters are the
authoritative per-code source and Prometheus is the cross-check, not the reverse.

3.3 PRE-GENERATE PAYLOADS AT STARTUP - AGREED, AND IT IS PROVABLY SAFE HERE.
Build 8 pre-serialized bytes bodies per (n, rejection-mix) pair at startup and
round-robin them, so the generator never serializes inside the request loop and
its own CPU does not become the bottleneck.

Reuse is valid because dedupe is PER BATCH: seen_order_ids is a local set created
fresh inside each process_batch call (worker/processing.py:34), so the same body
submitted a thousand times yields identical and correct rejection counts every
time. Write that down, because if dedupe were global, reuse would silently
corrupt the test and someone would eventually "improve" the generator into doing
it.

Two mechanical points. Content-Length must be exact, which pre-serialized bytes
give for free and which the middleware reads before parsing (api/main.py:154-162).
Memory: 8 bodies at n = 50,000 is about 34 MB - exactly 8 x 4,250,013 =
34,000,104 B, which was right - and all five C1 ladder sizes resident at once is
68.7 MB. [measured] [was] "about 40 MB", wrong by 1.7x: 8 bodies at each of
n = 1,000 / 5,000 / 15,000 / 30,000 / 50,000 is 8 x (85,013 + 425,013 +
1,275,013 + 2,550,013 + 4,250,013) = 68,680,520 B. Every (n, rejection-mix) pair
the whole plan needs, D2's 60%-malformed n = 1,000 and D's 25,000 included, is
86,314,972 B = 86.3 MB, measured by serializing them. All three are comfortable
inside a 2 GiB pod, so the error never changed a decision - but 40 MB is what a
later reader would have sized a smaller pod against, and 86.3 MB is the number
that actually bounds it.

One honest caveat: 8 identically shaped bodies give the api perfect branch
prediction and cache behaviour, so measured per-request CPU is a slight
UNDER-estimate of real-world cost. Varying the 8 by order_id range, region
string and which records are malformed keeps that small.


4. INJECTION POINT - CONFIRMED, PLUS THE PART THAT IS NOT OBVIOUS

The choice holds. allow-api-ingress (11-networkpolicy.yaml:10-27) selects
podSelector app=api and permits TCP 8000 from namespaceSelector
kubernetes.io/metadata.name=monitoring. That label is applied automatically by
the API server to every namespace, so the monitoring namespace Helm creates
(main_platform.tf:253-254) matches it with no manifest change. The rejected
alternatives are rejected for the right reasons: a bastion cannot reach a
ClusterIP and its source IP matches no selector, and port-forward is a single TCP
stream pinned to one pod.

Three consequences that are easy to get wrong.

4.1 NAMESPACE IS NOT NODE PLACEMENT. The policy selects on namespace; where the
pod runs is independent. A generator with no nodeSelector schedules onto an
untainted Karpenter ON-DEMAND node - consuming the very 16-CPU budget being
measured and competing with api pods for it. It must be pinned off that pool.

5. THE RAMP SHAPE, AND THE REASONING FOR IT

SEQUENCE FIRST, THEN TOGETHER. Three reasons.

(1) They confound. Request rate drives api CPU and therefore the HPA; batch size
drives per-message worker time; queue depth drives KEDA - but batch size ALSO
drives per-request api CPU, because Pydantic validation is O(n) in the record
count. Ramping both at once means a ceiling appears with no way to attribute it
to either axis.

(2) The batch-size effect is nonlinear and would swamp the rate axis. At
n = 50,000 the per-request api CPU is roughly 50x that at n = 1,000 [estimate],
so a combined ramp is really a batch-size ramp wearing a rate ramp's clothes.

(3) But a combined stage is still REQUIRED, because the two halves share one RDS
instance, one NAT, one OTel collector, one Fluent Bit DaemonSet and two node
pools. Cross-half contention only exists when both are hot, and that is the only
stage in which an RDS ceiling can realistically appear.

STEP LENGTH: 5 MINUTES for any step whose result depends on autoscaling.
Derived from the slowest link in a scale-up: metrics-server resolution about 15s,
HPA sync 15s, pod created, then if no node capacity exists a Karpenter NodeClaim
at 45-90s [estimate], an ECR pull at 20-60s [estimate], and readiness at
initialDelaySeconds 5 plus periodSeconds 10 (03-api-deployment.yaml:60-65) -
about 2-3 minutes worst case. Add the for: 5m and [5m] windows in
13-prometheus-rules.yaml:42-50 and 5 minutes is the shortest step that both
reaches steady state and can fire an alert.

STAGE A - GENERATOR SELF-CALIBRATION. 12.1 min. Zero AWS cost.
[was] "10 min. GET a non-existent path (/__loadgen_ping, which returns 404) at
200, 500, 1,000, 2,000 and 4,000 req/s, 45s each." That ladder cannot be run as
written - it exhausts the conntrack table on the generator's node partway
through the 2,000 req/s rung, and it does so in the one way that would invert
the result rather than degrade it. The derivation is CONNTRACK below. The rungs
that are shipped, in loadtest/config-calibration.json:

     200 req/s    45s
     idle        130s
     500 req/s    45s
     idle        130s
   1,000 req/s    45s
     idle        130s
   2,000 req/s    45s
     idle        130s
   4,000 req/s    25s
   ------------------
   total         725s

GET a non-existent path (/__loadgen_ping, which returns 404). No request body,
no S3, no SQS, no DB. This measures two things: the generator's own ceiling, and
the api's framework floor - FastAPI plus uvicorn plus OTel per-request overhead
with the handler's work removed. Subtracting that floor from Stage B's
per-request CPU decomposes the api cost, which no other stage can do.
Use a 404 path rather than /healthz deliberately: /healthz is the liveness probe
path (03-api-deployment.yaml:66-71, timeoutSeconds defaulting to 1,
failureThreshold 3), so hammering it can time the probe out and restart the
container. If you do want the router-matched path measured, run one short
/healthz step and watch RESTARTS - a restart there is a real finding about the
liveness probe sharing a loaded path, not a test artifact.
Stage A is also a free rehearsal of the entire HPA path, since 404s still burn
api CPU, at no AWS cost whatsoever.

CONNTRACK FORCED THE GAPS AND THE SHORT TOP RUNG. [derived] Keep-alive is off
(3.1), so every request is a new 5-tuple, and each one holds a conntrack entry
on the generator's node for 120s after it closes (3.1b, corrected).
Entries alive at t are every connection opened in (t - 120, t]. Integrating the
ORIGINAL back-to-back ladder against nf_conntrack_max = 131,072:

   t = 135.0s   end of the 1,000 rung        73,500     56% of the table
   t = 167.4s   32.4s into the 2,000 rung   131,100     TABLE FULL
   t = 180.0s   end of the 2,000 rung       150,000    114%
   t = 225.0s   end of the 4,000 rung       300,000    229%

The table fills at t = 167.4s, 12.6 seconds before the 4,000 rung begins at
t = 180s. That is why checking the top rung alone misses it: the rung that
breaks the run is the 2,000 one, and it breaks because of what the 500 and
1,000 rungs left behind in the window.

And the failure mode is the worst one available to this test. A full conntrack
table drops SYNs. The generator sees no HTTP status at all and records a CONNECT
TIMEOUT - and 3.2 names connect timeout as THE genuine "offered load exceeds
capacity" signal, the one that means uvicorn's accept backlog is full. A
node-local resource limit on the generator's own node would be read as the api
saturating. That is not a degraded measurement, it is an inverted one, and it
would be recorded as the headline finding of the run.

The 130s gaps let each rung's 120s window decay completely before the next
starts, so every rung stands alone and its peak is just rate x duration:

     200 x 45 =    9,000     6.9% of the table
     500 x 45 =   22,500    17.2%
   1,000 x 45 =   45,000    34.3%
   2,000 x 45 =   90,000    68.7%
   4,000 x 25 =  100,000    76.3%      45s here would be 180,000 = 137%

Only the top rung still needed shortening. 25s holds it at 76%.

THE COST OF THE SHORT RUNG, STATED PLAINLY. PodMonitors scrape at 30s
(08-podmonitor.yaml), so a 25s rung yields one scrape or none. The generator's
own numbers for that rung are unaffected - it writes gen.csv every second - but
the api-side per-pod CPU at 4,000 req/s will be thin to absent, and that is
exactly the point on the curve Stage A exists to pin down.

THE ALTERNATIVE, WHICH IS BETTER IF YOU WILL TAKE IT. Raise nf_conntrack_max on
the single monitoring node before T0 and set the top rung's duration_s back to
45. 262,144 carries 45s at 4,000 req/s with 31% headroom. It is one sysctl on
one node, it is reversible, and it buys back the only measurement this ladder
gives up. The shipped config takes the no-touch option because it must be safe
to apply without a node change, not because it is the better experiment.

[verify] BEFORE T0, on the monitoring node - the whole ladder shape rests on two
values that are assumed defaults:
   sysctl net.netfilter.nf_conntrack_max
   sysctl net.netfilter.nf_conntrack_tcp_timeout_time_wait
and watch node_nf_conntrack_entries against node_nf_conntrack_entries_limit on
the timeline throughout the run (3.1b). If entries approach the limit during any
rung, that rung's connect-timeout count is not a finding about the api.

STAGE C2a - KEDA COLD START. 10 min. RUNS BEFORE STAGE B, because it needs the
worker genuinely at 0 replicas, which only holds before any sustained load.
With the worker idle past cooldownPeriod 120, inject 500 messages at n = 1,000 as
fast as the api will take them (about 2s at 250 req/s), then stop dead. 500 is
comfortably above the roughly 100 needed to reach maxReplicaCount (2.7), so this
exercises the full 0-to-20 ramp. Measures: notice latency, the 0-to-1 activation,
the ramp shape (the KEDA-managed HPA's default scale-up policy is max(4 pods,
100%) per 15s), whether 20 is actually reached, the drain rate at 20 replicas,
and then the whole scale-down and return to zero.

STAGE B - RATE RAMP AT FIXED n = 1,000. 45 min.
83 KiB bodies: large enough that Pydantic validation is a measurable share of
per-request CPU, small enough that worker time stays around 45 ms [estimate] so
the queue stays shallow at the low end.
Baseline 5 req/s for 5 min, then 20, 50, 100, 200, 350, 500, 700, 900 req/s at
5 min each.
Ladder rationale: 3 pods x 700m = 2,100m of CPU before the HPA moves, so with
per-request CPU of X ms the first scale-up lands at about 2,100/X req/s - 210
req/s at X = 10 ms, 105 at X = 20 ms [derived]. The interesting band is 100-500
and the ladder brackets it. The top is deliberately placed past the estimated
10-pod ceiling (roughly 700-1,000 req/s at X = 10 ms [estimate]) so the ladder is
guaranteed to cross it rather than stopping just short.
Per-step stop rule: if the 202 rate stops tracking the offered rate by more than
5%, or generator CPU exceeds 85% of its limit, HOLD the step and record. Do not
escalate into a measurement that cannot be attributed.
Expect KEDA to scale during Stage B as well, and do not try to prevent it -
changing maxReplicaCount would be a mechanism change and a Git commit through
ArgoCD. Stage B's ceiling claim is about the API only, evidenced by per-pod CPU,
HPA desired versus max, and 202-rate versus offered-rate. If the backlog grows
without bound at the top of the ladder, that is not noise; see 6.4.

STAGE C1 - WORKER COST CURVE. 25 min plus about 10 min of settling after B.
Fixed low MESSAGE rate so exactly one worker is involved and neither autoscaler
moves: n = 1,000 / 5,000 / 15,000 at 2 req/s, then n = 30,000 / 50,000 at 1
req/s, 4 min each. At n = 50,000 one worker drains about 2.2 msg/s [estimate], so
1 req/s keeps visible depth at or below 4 and KEDA's desired stays at 1; if it
scales to 2, halve the rate and redo the step. On the api side, n = 50,000 at
1 req/s is about 0.4 CPU-seconds/s across 3 pods [estimate], so the HPA also
stays put - the stage is clean on both halves.
Deliverables: worker_processing_seconds p50/p95 per n (buckets run to 30s,
worker.py:36), peak worker RSS per n, and - because the api rate is fixed while n
varies - the per-record slope of api CPU.

STAGE C2b - KEDA EQUILIBRIUM. 20 min. Sustained 150 msg/s at n = 1,000 for

about 4 x 8 = 32 (2.7). A divergence from 4W falsifies the scaleOnInFlight

STAGE D - COMBINED. 25 min. Rate at 70% of Stage B's MEASURED api ceiling, with a

6. WHICH CEILING FIRST, AND HOW THE RESULTS TELL THEM APART

6.1 RANKED PREDICTION
(1) api CPU driving the HPA to maxReplicas 10. The HPA starts moving in the
    100-350 req/s band; the cap is reached somewhere in 700-1,000 req/s offered
    [estimate].
(2) The on-demand NodePool's 16-CPU limit, essentially simultaneously - a coin
    flip on Karpenter's instance choice (2.6).
(3) RDS appears as rising commit latency long before either cap, and converts
    into api 500s (QueuePool timeout) only at the very top. Multi-AZ
    (main_data.tf:201) means every commit waits on the standby's WAL write, which
    is a hard serialization point on a 2-vCPU burstable instance. RDS T3/T4g
    classes run in unlimited mode by default, so the instance will DEGRADE
    SMOOTHLY and bill for surplus CPU rather than throttle. Do not expect a knee.
(4) KEDA's maxReplicaCount 20, well before the spot pool's 32 CPU (2.5).
(5) The generator itself - the one failure that would silently invalidate
    everything, which is why Stage A exists and why generator CPU is a
    first-class series in the timeline.

6.2 DISCRIMINATION TABLE - what each ceiling looks like in the data

HPA CAP
  primary:      kubectl -n sales-pipeline get hpa api shows REPLICAS 10 and
                condition ScalingLimited=True with reason TooManyReplicas; ALL
                TEN PODS ARE Running; per-pod CPU pinned at about 1000m.
  corroborating: container_cpu_cfs_throttled_seconds_total climbing (requests ==
                limits, so throttling is certain at the cap); KubeHpaMaxedOut
                firing; 202 rate flat while offered rate keeps rising.

NODEPOOL CPU LIMIT
  primary:      api pods PENDING with FailedScheduling; kubectl get nodepool
                on-demand -o jsonpath='{.status.resources.cpu}' equals 16; the
                Karpenter controller log says the pool would exceed its limits.
  corroborating: KubeDeploymentReplicasMismatch and KubePodNotReady; HPA desired
                greater than current with no pod ever reaching Ready.
  This is the row that distinguishes it from the HPA cap: at the HPA cap every
  pod runs; at the NodePool limit some pod never schedules.

RDS
  primary:      RDS CloudWatch CPUUtilization, DBLoad, WriteLatency,
                DatabaseConnections; api p99 latency rising while api CPU is NOT
                pinned; api log line "failed to record job: QueuePool limit ...
                timed out" producing 500s (api/main.py:226-227).
  corroborating: Performance Insights top SQL is the jobs INSERT; commit latency
                tracks the Multi-AZ round trip.

KEDA CAP
  primary:      kubectl get hpa keda-hpa-worker shows 20/20; visible queue depth
                grows on a straight line at a constant slope.
  corroborating: spot NodePool .status.resources.cpu far below 32 (2.5).

GENERATOR
  primary:      generator container CPU at or above 90% of its limit; offered
                rate plateaus while client p50 latency stays flat;
                offered_dropped greater than zero.
  corroborating: api CPU BELOW the HPA target and the HPA not scaling - the tell
                that the client, not the system, is the bottleneck.

6.3 TWO SECONDARY CEILINGS WORTH WATCHING FOR

READINESS CASCADE. /readyz (api/main.py:189-201) makes three network calls - a DB
SELECT 1 through the SHARED pool, an SQS get_queue_attributes and an S3
head_bucket - with timeoutSeconds defaulting to 1 and failureThreshold 3
(03-api-deployment.yaml:60-65). Under pool exhaustion it will exceed 1s, pods go
NotReady after about 30s, the Service endpoint set shrinks, and the survivors get
worse. Track READY ENDPOINT COUNT as a first-class series. If every replica is
unavailable for 5 minutes, APIUnavailable fires
(13-prometheus-rules.yaml:51-58) - a real outcome, not a false alarm. Liveness is
safe: /healthz returns a static dict and touches nothing (api/main.py:184-186),
so pods will not be killed by this.

THE OBSERVABILITY PATH SATURATING. The api's OTel auto-instrumentation exports a
span per request to a single-replica collector with a 250m CPU limit and a
memory_limiter at 80% (12-otel-collector.yaml:16-22,31-36), feeding Tempo at 500m
(main_platform.tf:541-555). At peak this will drop spans. That is a finding about
the tracing path's capacity, NOT a reason to disable instrumentation - production
runs with it, so the test must too, and its per-request overhead is part of what
is being measured. Same for Fluent Bit: at peak the pipeline emits roughly 1,300
log lines/s (one per accepted job at api/main.py:242, one per processed message at
worker.py:205-214) into a 200m CPU DaemonSet. Dropped lines are a finding.

6.4 THE FINDING I MOST EXPECT
api ingest capacity at 10 pods (roughly 700-1,000 req/s) against worker drain
capacity at 20 replicas (roughly 400 msg/s at n = 1,000) [estimate] - a 1.5x to
2x MISMATCH IN FAVOUR OF INGEST. Nothing is lost, because retention is 4 days
(main_data.tf:108), but the pipeline would accept work about twice as fast as its
maximum drain rate. If that holds, it is the most actionable number in the run,
and Stage E measures the drain side directly rather than by estimate.

6.5 SCALE-DOWN, OBSERVED RATHER THAN ASSUMED
Four independent mechanisms, in sequence. Record the wall-clock of each
transition; the INTERVALS are the deliverable, not the end state.

(1) BACKLOG DRAIN. Load stops, workers stay at 20 and drain. N/T from the
    observer's queue-depth series IS the authoritative worker fleet drain rate -
    the cleanest measurement in the whole plan, and it comes free from the
    scale-down stage.
(2) API HPA. The default downscale stabilization window is 300s, after which the
    default policy permits halving every 15s, so 10 -> 5 -> 3 within about 30-45s
    of the window closing. Predicted: first down-step at load_stop + about 300s,
    back to 3 by about +375s [derived]. The HPA's own AbleToScale condition
    message names the stabilization window explicitly - capture it verbatim.
(3) WORKER: HPA THEN KEDA. The ScaledObject declares no
    advanced.horizontalPodAutoscalerConfig.behavior

(4) KARPENTER. consolidateAfter 1m with budget nodes "10%"; 10% of 4 nodes rounds

7. THE OBSERVER

The priority is a correlated timeline of load-sent versus system-response, not
latency percentiles. So: TWO CSVs JOINED ON EPOCH SECONDS, plus a run manifest.
Percentiles are recorded but secondary.

timeline.csv - 10 SECOND CADENCE, written from the operator's workstation through
the SSM tunnel (RUNBOOK "Accessing the private cluster"). 10s resolves a 15s HPA
step and a 30s KEDA poll without being noisy. Five API calls per tick, assembled
by jq into one row, about 1s of work per tick:

  ts,api_desired,api_ready,api_pending,api_endpoints_ready,
  hpa_api_cur_pct,hpa_api_desired,hpa_api_limited,
  worker_desired,worker_ready,worker_pending,keda_hpa_cur,keda_hpa_desired,
  sqs_visible,sqs_notvisible,dlq_visible,
  nodes_ondemand,nodes_spot,nodeclaims_notready,np_od_cpu,np_spot_cpu,
  api_cpu_sum_m,api_cpu_max_m,api_mem_max_mi,
  worker_cpu_sum_m,worker_mem_max_mi,gen_cpu_m,gen_mem_mi,
  sample_ok

Two caveats to keep with the file so the plot is not misread. The CPU and memory
columns come from metrics-server, whose resolution is about 15s, so they LAG
their own row's timestamp by up to 15 seconds - which matters on a 10s timeline.
And sample_ok flags ticks where a call failed: the SSM tunnel can drop, so the
observer must APPEND, leaving a visible gap on reconnect rather than losing the
file.

gen.csv - 1 SECOND CADENCE, written by the generator to STDOUT so Fluent Bit
ships it to CloudWatch and it survives the pod dying. One line per second is
free:

  ts,stage,offered,sent,offered_dropped,inflight,
  h202,h400,h411,h413,h422,h500,h502,hother,
  e_conn_refused,e_conn_timeout,e_read_timeout,e_reset,
  lat_p50_ms,lat_p95_ms,lat_max_ms,bytes_sent,records_sent

gen-requests.ndjson - PER REQUEST, to the pod's emptyDir:
{ts, job_id, status, n, ms}. Retrieved at the end with kubectl cp. This is what
makes the DB-side timeline reconstruction in section 8 possible - job_id is the
join key, and no off-the-shelf load tool produces it, which is the decisive reason
to use a purpose-built generator rather than vegeta or hey. If disk or CPU
becomes a problem, fall back to sampling 1 request in 10; that is a pre-planned
lever and must be recorded in the manifest when used, never applied silently.

events.log - the operator's own timestamped notes, one line per observation
(echo "$(date -u +%s) ..."). Stage boundaries and anything surprising.

run-manifest.json - captured at T0 BEFORE any load: every value in section 1 as
read from the LIVE cluster, plus the deployed image SHA, the region, the
Karpenter instance types actually in use, max_connections, the kube-proxy mode,
the Service's sessionAffinity, and the jobs/results row counts and epoch second
that form the reconciliation baseline.

POST-RUN PULLS, NOT LIVE SAMPLERS, for RDS: CloudWatch backfills, so sampling
live buys nothing and costs API calls. One aws cloudwatch get-metric-data over
the whole window at 60s period for CPUUtilization, DatabaseConnections,
WriteLatency, ReadLatency, FreeableMemory, WriteIOPS and DBLoad. SQS is the
opposite case and IS sampled live: get-queue-attributes is near-real-time and far
better than CloudWatch's 1-5 minute granularity for queue depth.

FROM PROMETHEUS AFTER THE RUN, as cross-check and not as source of truth (2.10):
  - sum by (pod) (rate(http_requests_total{handler="/jobs"}[1m])) - the per-pod
    distribution, i.e. the proof that disabling keep-alive worked;
  - container_cpu_cfs_throttled_seconds_total for both workloads;
  - container_memory_max_usage_bytes - a since-container-start HIGH-WATER MARK,
    so it captures a peak that a 30s scrape would miss. This is the correct way
    to re-derive docs/DESIGN.md:130-132's 446 MiB and 106 MiB figures. [verify]
    that the kubelet's cadvisor endpoint exposes it;
  - node_nf_conntrack_entries and node_nf_conntrack_entries_limit (3.1b);
  - ALERTS{alertstate="firing"} with timestamps.

THE PRIMARY VIEW is one stacked plot sharing a single x-axis with stage
boundaries marked:
  top    - offered / 202 / each error class / offered_dropped
  middle - api desired vs ready vs pending, ready endpoint count, on-demand node
           count
  bottom - SQS visible plus in-flight, worker desired vs ready, spot node count
  strip  - generator CPU, which is the honesty check on every row above it.


8. WHERE SMOKE.SH FITS AS A CORRECTNESS ORACLE

BEFORE, AGAINST DOCKER-COMPOSE - WHERE IT WAS DESIGNED TO RUN.
docker compose up, then ./smoke.sh (CLAUDE.md "Commands"), plus the three test
files. This proves the CODE computes 350.5 total, 3 processed and 3 rejected
(smoke.sh:60-63) before the cluster gets blamed for anything. Unmodified, in its
intended environment, where its 20s readiness and 30s job-poll budgets are
correct because localstack and a local Postgres answer immediately. This is
pre-flight step 1 and costs nothing.

AFTER, IN-CLUSTER - UNMODIFIED, BUT PRE-WARMED.
2.8 has the arithmetic: 15 iterations of sleep 2 (about 30s, smoke.sh:38-54)
against a 90-195s cold KEDA scale-from-zero is a guaranteed false-fail. Do not
change smoke.sh - it is the pre-flight oracle and its docker-compose timings are
correct for that job. Remove the cold start instead:
  1. submit one throwaway batch from the generator pod;
  2. kubectl -n sales-pipeline wait --for=condition=Ready pod -l app=worker
     --timeout=300s
  3. kubectl -n sales-pipeline port-forward deploy/api 18000:8000 &
  4. API_URL=http://localhost:18000 ./smoke.sh
With a warm worker already blocked in a 20s long poll (worker.py:243-247), SQS
delivers immediately and the job reaches done in about 2-4s - two of the fifteen
available poll iterations.

Port-forward is the right tool HERE and the wrong tool for load, exactly as the
brief says: it pins to one pod, which is fine for a correctness assertion and
fatal for a distribution measurement. It should work despite default-deny-ingress,
because the kubelet's readiness and liveness probes already reach port 8000 from
the node, which proves node-sourced traffic to that port is permitted. [verify]
If it turns out to be blocked, fall back to a curl-capable pod in the monitoring
namespace issuing the same three requests.

RECONCILIATION IS THE ORACLE THAT ACTUALLY SCALES. smoke.sh checks six records;
this checks a million and a half, from one psql session (section 14):

  SELECT count(*) FILTER (WHERE status='pending') AS pending,
         count(*) FILTER (WHERE status='done')    AS done,
         count(*) FILTER (WHERE status='failed')  AS failed,
         count(*)                                  AS jobs
    FROM jobs WHERE submitted_at >= to_timestamp(:t0);

  SELECT count(*) AS results, sum(rows_processed) AS processed,
         sum(rows_rejected) AS rejected
    FROM results r JOIN jobs j USING (job_id)
   WHERE j.submitted_at >= to_timestamp(:t0);

Assertions: jobs equals the generator's 202 count; done equals results;
processed + rejected equals the sum of n over accepted jobs; pending is 0 after
the drain; failed is 0 - and any non-zero failed is a finding, with the api and
worker log lines saying which of the two _mark_failed paths caused it
(api/main.py:246-258, worker/worker.py:222-234).

Because the jobs table carries submitted_at and completed_at
(api/main.py:86-92) and the worker sets completed_at on first write
(worker.py:136-140), the per-job end-to-end latency for EVERY job comes from one
query after the run. That is why the plan does NOT poll /jobs/{id} during the
run: polling would add api and RDS load and distort the very measurement being
taken.

And the idempotency proof under real load, free from the metrics:
worker_jobs_processed_total{outcome="redelivery_skipped"} greater than zero means
SQS redelivered and the ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING path absorbed it - the design
claim at docs/DESIGN.md:32-38, demonstrated rather than asserted.

11. WHAT THIS TEST CANNOT TELL YOU

  - NOTHING ABOUT A PUBLIC ENTRY POINT, because there is not one. ALB, WAF, a
    custom domain, ACM and Cognito are all deliberately deferred
    (docs/DESIGN.md:144-152). The measured api ceiling excludes any
    ingress-layer cost, so it is not a production capacity number for a
    public-facing version of this system.
  - NOTHING ABOUT MAX_S3_BYTES. 2.2 proves the API cannot produce an object large
    enough to trip it. The worker's 15 MiB guard stays untested by this run and
    must not be reported otherwise.
  - NOTHING ABOUT RESILIENCE. No Spot interruption, no AZ loss, no NAT failure.
    With az_count = 2 and single_nat = true (terraform/variables.tf:18-34) those
    failure modes exist and this plan does not touch them. A deliberate Spot
    interruption is a separate test, and it is the one that would actually
    exercise the idempotency argument in docs/DESIGN.md:82-86.
  - NOTHING ABOUT SUSTAINED BEHAVIOUR. Three and a half hours says nothing about
    memory growth over days, autovacuum and bloat on a jobs table taking 1.5M
    rows per run, log-group cost drift, S3 lifecycle behaviour, or T4g credit
    dynamics over a week.
  - NOTHING ABOUT THE READ PATH. /jobs/{id} and /jobs/{id}/result polling is
    deliberately excluded to keep the write-path measurement clean (section 8),
    so the run does not measure read cost or the share of the 15-connection
    per-pod DB pool that real polling clients would take. If clients poll, the
    connection and CPU budgets in 2.4 change.
  - WHERE THE API'S CPU ACTUALLY GOES. It finds WHERE the ceiling is, not WHAT to
    optimise. Splitting per-request CPU across Pydantic validation, boto3 request
    signing, uvicorn and the OTel span needs a profiler, and that is not a small
    add: py-spy needs SYS_PTRACE, which the restricted Pod Security Admission
    labels on the namespace forbid (00-namespace.yaml:7-11). Stage A's framework
    floor is the only decomposition this plan delivers.
  - WHETHER THE CPU HPA IS THE RIGHT SIGNAL. It shows what the HPA DOES. The
    decision needs the latency-versus-CPU curve this run produces plus a
    judgement call; the run is the input to that decision, not the answer. The
    same is true of whether a concurrency limit is warranted - this run tells you
    what happens without one.
  - THAT ALERTS WHICH DID NOT FIRE WOULD WORK. It exercises the SNS path for the
    rules that fire. WorkerProcessingSlow, APIScrapeMissing and, if the readiness
    cascade does not happen, APIUnavailable all stay unverified.
  - PORTABILITY OF ANY NUMBER HERE. They are specific to the region, the AZ pair,
    the instance types Karpenter chose, and this image SHA. If Karpenter picks a
    different family next time, the per-pod ceiling moves and 2.6's coin flip may
    land the other way. That is why the run manifest records instance types.

                      "effect":"NoSchedule"}]}}' \
    -- psql "host=$DBHOST port=5432 dbname=pipeline user=pipeline sslmode=require" -At \
       -c "show max_connections" \
       -c "select count(*) from pg_stat_activity" \
       -c "select count(*) from jobs" \
       -c "select count(*) from results" \
       -c "select extract(epoch from now())::bigint"

The password lands in the pod spec, so it is readable by anyone with pod-read
RBAC in that namespace. Acceptable for a pre-production system, and the same
shape as RUNBOOK Phase 1.5's psql pod (RUNBOOK.md:217-223), but delete the pod
when done (--rm covers the happy path only) and do not reuse the shell.
The nodeSelector plus toleration matter: without them the pod lands on a
Karpenter on-demand node and consumes the 16-CPU budget being measured (4.1).
The DB SG admits the private-subnet CIDRs (main_data.tf:165-179), which every
pod IP comes from under the VPC CNI, so an in-cluster pod reaches it and a
laptop over the NAT does not.

WHAT WAS MEASURED, AND HOW
All of it on a workstation against a local reference target, NOT on this
cluster. The AWS-side numbers in this plan are untouched and still [derived].
  - Client cost per fresh-connection request, one CPython core: raw
    asyncio.Protocol 185 us, asyncio streams 213 us, threads + http.client
    1,412 us at 2.28 cores. That is why the generator is two asyncio worker
    processes over raw protocols and not a thread pool, and why one process
    cannot carry 4,000 req/s.
  - The shipped generator at the Stage A rung rates, all with zero
    offered_dropped and zero transport errors:
        1,000 req/s   delivered   995.2/s    615m steady
        2,000 req/s   delivered 1,990.5/s    670m steady,   788m peak
        4,000 req/s   delivered 3,966.2/s    980m steady, 1,373m peak
        /jobs 100/s at n = 1,000               140m steady
    Peak RSS 28 + 24 + 24 MiB across parent and two workers.
  - TIME_WAIT sides at 4,000 conn/s: 14,107 server, 0 client. 3.1b confirmed.
  - Payload correctness against the repository's real worker/processing.py
    process_batch: for every (n, reject_frac) tried, all 8 bodies produce
    identical rows_processed and rows_rejected, matching what the generator
    predicts and prints at startup. n = 1,000 with no rejections serialises to
    exactly 85,013 bytes, which is 2.1's arithmetic to the byte.

WHAT THAT SETTLED ABOUT 4.2's POD
requests == limits, cpu 2, memory 1Gi, ephemeral-storage 1Gi. 4,000 req/s costs
1,373m at peak, which is 69% of a 2-CPU limit and therefore clear of the 85%
HOLD rule in Stage B. At 1,500m it would be 92% and the generator would trip its
own honesty rule at the top rung.

4.2's HEADROOM FIGURE WAS OPTIMISTIC AND IS NOW CORRECTED IN PLACE. 4.2 said
